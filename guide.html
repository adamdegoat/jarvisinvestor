◇ How Jarvis Investor works · and how it grades itself — the honest version
1 · We gather the facts — mechanically. Every run, plain Python pulls real prices for the indices, the 11 sectors, commodities, key macro rates, and ~40 liquid stocks. No opinions at this stage — just data, including each stock's relative strength vs the S&P 500.
2 · We reason over it — in plain English. An AI analyst reads that data (and its own past scorecard) and writes the day's view: the market regime, what's driving it, sectors to consider or be cautious on, commodities, and a few stocks — each with a confidence level and a reason a normal person can follow.
3 · We turn every view into a dated, falsifiable call. A sector we say to consider becomes a recorded prediction: “expect this to beat the S&P over the next ~30 days.” Caution = expect it to lag. A constructive commodity = expect it up. We log the price the moment we make the call — no rewriting history.
4 · Reality grades us. After ~30 days, each call is scored automatically against what actually happened — beat the benchmark (win), lagged (loss), or roughly matched (neutral). No cherry-picking; every call counts.
5 · We learn from the scorecard. The hit rates feed back in. If our “High conviction” calls aren't actually winning more than our “Low” ones, the system tightens what earns high conviction. If one kind of call (say single stocks) grades worse, it carries more humility there. That feedback loop is the whole point — it's built to get smarter over time, in public.
◇ Investing principles for everyday investors · timeless, boring, and the stuff that actually matters
1. The trend is your friend — until it bends
Most of the time, going with the prevailing direction beats fighting it. We lean on trend and relative strength rather than guessing tops and bottoms. But trends do end, which is why every call has an exit condition ("what would change my mind").
2. Don't chase what's already tripled
A stock that's up 150% in three months can keep rising — or fall 30% in a week. Buying purely because something is "up a lot" is how everyday investors get hurt. Strong and not-too-stretched beats strongest-at-any-price.
3. Diversify, especially when one area is winning
When a single sector (like Big Tech) is driving the whole market, it's tempting to pile in. That's exactly when concentration risk is highest. Spreading across sectors smooths the ride.
4. Size your bets so no single one can ruin you
How much you put in any one idea matters more than the idea itself. Position sizing — keeping any one bet small enough that being wrong is survivable — is the single most underrated skill in investing.
5. Time in the market usually beats timing the market
Trying to jump in and out perfectly is extremely hard and most people do it badly. A steady, diversified approach over years tends to win. Use our read to tilt and stay informed — not to flip your whole portfolio on a dime.
6. Nobody is right every time — and anyone who claims to be is lying
Even a great process loses regularly. That's why we publish our hit rate openly and grade ourselves against reality. Judge any source (including us) by their track record over time, not their loudest call.
◇ Glossary · every term we use, in plain words
Sector ETF
A single fund that holds all the big companies in one part of the economy — e.g. XLK is technology, XLE is energy. Watching sector ETFs tells you which parts of the market are leading and which are lagging, without picking individual stocks.
Benchmark
The yardstick you measure against. For US stocks it's almost always the S&P 500 (the 500 largest US companies). "Beating the benchmark" means doing better than the overall market.
Relative strength
How a stock or sector is doing compared to the market, not on its own. A stock can be up 5% but still weak if the market is up 10%. We grade our calls on relative strength because beating the market is the real test.
Alpha
The extra return above the benchmark. If the S&P rose 6% and your stock rose 16%, that 10% of out-performance is the alpha. It's relative strength expressed as a number.
Moving average (50- / 200-day)
The average closing price over the last 50 or 200 days, drawn as a smooth line. Price above the 200-day line = a longer-term uptrend; below = a downtrend. It's a simple, widely-watched trend filter.
VIX (the "fear gauge")
A measure of how much volatility traders expect in the next month. Low (under ~16) = calm, complacent markets; high (over ~25) = fear and stress. It tends to spike when markets fall.
Yield curve (10Y–2Y)
The gap between long-term and short-term government interest rates. Normally long rates are higher. When it goes negative ("inverted"), it has historically been an early warning of recession — though often months ahead.
Risk-on / Risk-off
Market mood. Risk-on = investors feel confident and buy growth/stocks. Risk-off = investors get defensive and move to safer assets (cash, bonds, gold). It frames everything else.
Momentum
The tendency of things that have been rising (or falling) to keep going in that direction for a while. "The trend is your friend" is the momentum idea in plain words.
Overextended / stretched
When a price has run up so far, so fast that it's well above its averages and due for a pause or pullback. A great company can still be a risky entry if it's overextended — that's why we flag it.
Defensive sectors
Parts of the market people need no matter what — utilities, consumer staples (food, household goods), healthcare. They tend to hold up better when markets fall, but lag when markets rip higher.
Diversification
Not putting all your eggs in one basket. Spreading money across different sectors and assets so one bad bet doesn't sink you. It matters most exactly when one corner of the market is doing all the work.
Hit rate
Of the calls we've made that have finished (matured), the share that turned out right. A 60% hit rate means 6 of every 10 graded calls won. We publish it openly — wins and misses both count.
Calibration
Whether our confidence matches reality. If our "High conviction" calls really do win more often than our "Low" ones, we're well-calibrated. If they don't, we're overconfident — and the system tightens its own standards.
Conviction (High / Medium / Low)
How sure we are about a call. It's not a promise — it's an honest signal of strength of evidence. Higher conviction should, over time, mean a higher hit rate. We track that to keep ourselves honest.
